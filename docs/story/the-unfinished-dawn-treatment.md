# The Unfinished Dawn

**Status:** Alternative story treatment, version 0.1. Full proposed-story spoilers.  
**Brief:** A cohesive party JRPG with loss, betrayal, discoveries about the past, exploration and a decisive confrontation with its main antagonist. Trades provide substantial optional progression.  
**Relationship to earlier work:** The Hearthroad, The Names We Keep and The Wandering Table remain available in the [original research document](../research/android-jrpg-market-and-story-foundations.md). This treatment explores the revised brief; it does not replace or approve any concept. Names and title are provisional.

## 1. The central story

Rowan Hale is a provincial courier who believes the kingdom's celebrated Lord Marshal, Sevren Veyr, brought peace to the country in which his father died. Rowan wants a place in the royal roadwardens: steady work, a uniform his mother could be proud of, and a chance to travel beyond the valley.

Then soldiers destroy his home while hunting a military physician. Rowan's older sister, Mira, dies helping people escape. Evidence leads him towards the same national hero he grew up admiring.

What begins as an attempt to bring one officer to justice becomes a struggle against Veyr's plan to control the kingdom's armies through an ancient Crown. Rowan discovers that his father's part in the last war was neither the heroic legend he inherited nor a simple story of innocent victimhood. Meanwhile, Cael, his closest friend and first companion, comes to believe that Veyr's enforced peace is preferable to another civil war.

Cael eventually betrays the party by returning the captured Crown to Veyr and delivering one of their allies into custody. Rowan must rebuild trust, assemble people with reasons to oppose Veyr, and learn how to defeat him without killing the soldiers bound to his power.

**The final confrontation is with Veyr himself.** He is not displaced by an unrelated god or a previously unmentioned mastermind. The resolution answers the argument that divided Rowan and Cael: whether preventing another tragedy justifies taking away everyone else's right to choose.

Mining, fishing, smithing and other trades exist throughout this adventure. The story still works if the player never becomes an expert in any of them.

## 2. What gives the adventure its identity

The emotional progression is **belonging → loss → pursuit → uncomfortable truth → betrayal → rebuilding trust → confrontation → a changed life**.

The story should contain funny travel conversations, beautiful places, local festivals, friendships and ordinary pleasures. Most settlements are functioning communities, not consecutive ruins. The player needs to experience what is worth protecting and have opportunities to enjoy it.

Its main dramatic question is personal before it becomes political: **when someone you love does something terrible, what does loving them require of you?** Rowan confronts different versions of that question through his father, Cael, his mother and eventually himself.

Veyr offers a seductive answer: stop people having the power to make disastrous choices. Rowan's answer has to be earned through admitting his own mistakes and trusting others, rather than delivering a speech about freedom while always acting correctly.

## 3. The protagonist's life before the plot

### Rowan Hale — courier, later a wayblade

Rowan is twenty-four and works the roads around **Brackenford**, a river crossing whose inn is run by his mother, Tessa, and older sister, Mira. He can fight because travelling couriers must protect themselves, not because he secretly belongs to a royal bloodline.

He likes finding shortcuts, remembers people by the parcels he has delivered, and is better at helping with a practical problem than talking about a painful one. His flaw is a belief that being useful means solving matters himself. Under pressure, that becomes recklessness and a refusal to admit that he needs help.

His father, Rellan, supposedly died defending civilians during the last civil war fourteen years earlier. Rowan built his idea of adulthood around that story. Cael, his childhood friend, shares the ambition to serve a kingdom they believe their parents saved.

Mira has a life beyond being Rowan's future loss. She handles difficult guests, argues about money, teaches local children to read between shifts and plans to turn an unused storeroom into a classroom. She loves Rowan but is tired of being treated as the person who will always stay behind to keep everything running.

### What the player actually does in the opening

An ordinary delivery route introduces the river landing, market, hillside path and inn. Rowan returns with news of his roadwarden interview. Mira asks who he thinks will cover his duties if he leaves; Cael responds by planning an extravagant farewell neither can afford.

The player helps resolve a missing-parcel dispute, fights a small roadside creature encounter with Cael, and joins preparations for a river festival. Optional stalls introduce fishing or simple material collection without requiring participation. These activities remain available elsewhere if skipped.

A visiting physician, Ysra, arrives early and privately asks Mira for shelter. Rowan recognises her from previous visits. The change in Mira's manner and the presence of unfamiliar soldiers provide warning without revealing the full threat.

We need enough playable normal life to understand these relationships before the attack. The opening should not be a prolonged tutorial with a tragedy attached at the end.

## 4. The antagonist and the rules of his power

### Lord Marshal Sevren Veyr

Veyr helped end the civil war and genuinely prevented further fighting in several regions. His legitimacy is therefore more substantial than propaganda alone. Merchants remember reopened roads; veterans remember that he brought them home.

He also authorised atrocities and suppressed evidence when it threatened the settlement he wanted. Over time, he turned a willingness to commit an exceptional wrong into a belief that only he could be trusted with the country's future.

His goal is to become sovereign and bind the royal armies to the **First Crown**, an artefact recovered within the past year. He plans to present the coronation and a grand military muster as the end of factional government. Once he controls the armies, compulsory civil oaths will follow.

His diagnosis contains truth: several rival leaders would restart the war for their own advantage. His conclusion is still wrong. He intends to decide which lives, loyalties and freedoms can be sacrificed, while making resistance impossible.

He recognises Rowan's grief and offers it a political use: help make a country in which no commander can ever disobey the order to keep peace. The offer omits the central problem—Veyr would decide what peace means and whom it excludes.

### Five rules the plot must respect

1. **The Crown binds prepared military oathmarks.** It cannot arbitrarily control an unmarked stranger, read minds or revive the dead. Preparation is a physical rite, which the army presents as an honour of service.
2. **A bearer wearing the Crown commands through consecrated regimental standards.** This makes military musters and control of the standards important. Taking off the Crown stops active commands, but does not erase the bindings.
3. **Destroying the Crown while bindings remain kills the people caught in them.** Removing or killing a bearer alone does not safely release those people; another bearer could resume control. This danger is established and demonstrated well before the finale.
4. **An older release rite can sever the bindings without killing their holders.** The party learns and tests it on a small scale. Releasing entire regiments requires access to their standards while the Crown is active. The finale applies an established solution under dangerous conditions.
5. **Ordinary characters remain responsible for ordinary decisions.** Cael's betrayal is voluntary. Veyr's past atrocities predate his discovery of the Crown. Magic does not retrospectively excuse either of them.

The Crown amplifies an existing conflict; it does not explain every historical event. Healing items restore people who are incapacitated in combat. They cannot reverse Mira's death or later turn grief into a resurrection errand.

## 5. The party: why they meet, why they stay

The proposed roster has five eventual permanent companions, plus Cael's substantial early role. This early treatment proposed three fighting at once; the [current design plan](../design/game-design-and-production-plan.md) proposes four, pending confirmation. Those numbers are provisional; each character's dramatic purpose matters more than roster size.

| Character | First meeting and reason to join | Personal conflict | Battle identity | Why they remain involved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rowan — Wayblade** | The playable protagonist; first seeks the officer responsible for Brackenford. | Confuses taking responsibility with personally controlling every outcome. | Fast sword attacks, counters and assisting an ally's opening. | The truth implicates his national hero and forces him to decide what kind of person he will become. |
| **Cael — Knight** | Childhood friend and opening companion; escapes Brackenford beside Rowan. | Cannot bear being powerless again; increasingly values certainty over consent. | Direct attacks, protection and strong finishing moves. | Initially seeks justice; later decides Veyr offers the only durable peace and betrays the group. |
| **Ysra — Ward Physician** | Reunited at a refugee infirmary in chapter 2; Rowan needs to know why soldiers pursued her. | Her attempt to expose military experiments brought danger to people who sheltered her. | Healing, cleansing and protective wards with limited offensive magic. | Patients remain captive, and silence would allow the experiments to become national policy. |
| **Nessa — Ranger** | A boat operator and smuggler recruited in chapter 3 to reach an island prison. | Protects her own small circle and distrusts causes that consume ordinary people. | Ranged attacks, traps, target marking and item support. | Her brother's rescue exposes a threat to every harbour community; she decides who she is willing to stand beside. |
| **Orin — Runesage** | A royal archivist encountered inside a military fortress in chapter 4. | Helped turn selective accounts into official history, although he did not know every atrocity. | Elemental magic, identifying enemy patterns and interrupting charged abilities. | His skills can expose the record and release the bound soldiers; withdrawal would repeat his earlier cowardice. |
| **Bram — Vanguard** | An opposing captain first fought in chapter 4; joins in chapter 7 after openly defying Veyr. | Wants coercion to excuse his entire career, including choices he made freely. | Interception, stagger and drawing attacks away from vulnerable allies. | He can help free his regiment, but must earn cooperation from people harmed by his service. |

### Relationships that develop between plot events

- **Rowan and Cael:** familiar teasing and automatic cooperation make the later division hurt. Their disagreements progress from methods to values; they do not suddenly become strangers when the plot needs a traitor.
- **Rowan and Ysra:** he initially wants a person to blame. She refuses to offer either a convenient excuse or a false apology for trying to save patients. Trust grows through action before full emotional reconciliation.
- **Nessa and Orin:** she mistrusts official histories; he initially mistakes her lack of formal education for ignorance. They become effective partners because she knows what his documents leave out.
- **Ysra and Bram:** she recognises the harm done to oathmarked soldiers but will not let him claim that all his conduct was compelled. Their eventual respect depends on his willingness to be specific about his choices.
- **Rowan and Bram:** accepting a former enemy after losing a friend tests whether Rowan can judge conduct rather than uniforms. Acceptance is cautious and earned.

Every companion should initiate at least one necessary main-story action. Essential motivations and resolutions belong on the main path. Optional companion quests add intimacy and alter personal epilogues; they must not contain the only explanation of why someone joined.

## 6. The campaign, in causal order

The chapter sequence is a full-story proposal, not a production schedule. Each destination follows from information, a person or a consequence established earlier.

### Act I — A name to blame

**Chapter 1: The river behind us — Brackenford.** Captain Hadrik arrives with a royal order to seize Ysra and her records. Mira refuses to hand over a wounded patient without explanation. Hadrik closes the crossing, orders a search that becomes a violent clearance, and has buildings burned to eliminate hiding places and evidence.

Rowan and Cael help evacuate people. Mira dies when the river stair collapses while she is helping families to a ferry. This is an authored event, not a punishment for failing a secret timer or choosing the wrong dialogue. The player knows what Rowan tried and why he could not reach her.

Rowan escapes with a copy of the seizure order. His mother survives among the refugees. The immediate goal is concrete: find Ysra, learn what Hadrik was hunting, and get the order before a magistrate who can act on it. Cael promises they will never be this helpless again.

**Chapter 2: The petition — Stoneharbour.** Rowan finds Ysra treating refugees. She explains that army patients were being subjected to an oath-binding procedure and that she took records to expose it. Mira had previously sheltered people Ysra helped, which explains why she trusted the inn; Brackenford was not chosen by fate or a secret bloodline.

They attempt the lawful route. The magistrate recognises the royal seal and orders them detained rather than investigate. Escaping the hearing establishes that the problem extends beyond Hadrik. Ysra joins to reach surviving patients whose testimony can substantiate the documents.

A prison transfer record points to the marsh islands. Stoneharbour becomes a recurring refuge and a practical home for shops, guilds and optional trades. Rowan can visit his mother between journeys; the family connection continues after the opening tragedy.

**Chapter 3: A boat for three promises — the salt marshes.** Nessa agrees to take them into an island prison if they also rescue her brother, Kellan, a dockworker detained after witnessing the transport of oathmarked soldiers. The party's objective and her personal need coincide without making them identical.

The breakout requires scouting tides, opening the prison's service route and dividing attention between prisoners and guards. Rowan abandons the agreed escape position to pursue Hadrik. He wins the confrontation, but the delay leaves Nessa's boat trapped and Kellan badly injured. Killing the immediate enemy does not produce the relief Rowan expected.

Nessa stays because other witnesses remain in danger and because she intends to see the larger operation stopped. She does not instantly forgive Rowan. Cael helps carry Kellan out and concludes that Rowan's grief makes him unreliable. The rescued records identify the fortress where the Crown is being prepared for transfer to the capital.

### Act II — What we inherit

**Chapter 4: The king who has not been crowned — Rookspine.** The party enters a mountain fortress to interrupt that transfer. Orin is there cataloguing recovered royal material. At first he tries to protect his position and avoid involvement; learning what is being done to the prisoners forces him to choose. He gives the party access and leaves with them, becoming publicly implicated.

Bram commands the garrison. The player encounters both his harsh ordinary orders and a distinct episode in which a trial warden briefly wears the Crown and activates the fortress standard, forcing marked soldiers to act. Their conduct is not all presented as the same thing. The party defeats the garrison leaders and takes the Crown, but cannot safely destroy it because the surviving test subjects remain bound.

Veyr, in the capital, offers negotiations through an envoy: return the artefact and he will recognise the survivors' claims and punish excessive force at Brackenford. Ysra has an authenticated instruction showing that he authorised the clearance himself. Rowan refuses. Cael hears Veyr's argument anyway: rival generals are already preparing another war, and the Crown could make that impossible.

Orin identifies the ruined judicial archive at Harrowfield as the best source for the earlier binding rites. The next journey is an attempt to render the captured weapon harmless, rather than a search for arbitrary keys.

**Chapter 5: The names beneath the monument — Harrowfield.** The archive lies beneath a city destroyed in the civil war. Exploration reveals ordinary lives alongside the political record: a schoolroom, a barracks, messages left at a sealed gate. The place has meaning before it supplies answers.

The party recovers a release rite and tests it successfully on a surviving test subject travelling with Ysra's patients. That establishes a hopeful, practical alternative to either using or smashing the Crown. It also reveals why releasing whole regiments will require access to their standards while the Crown is active.

Separately, records expose Rellan Hale's history. Rowan's father helped enforce the city's enclosure before refusing the order to burn an occupied district. He opened an escape route and was killed by his own side. He was neither innocent of everything nor incapable of choosing differently.

Orin recognises how the official account was edited. He helped preserve that version years later, without checking the surviving testimony. He admits what he knew and what he failed to ask. Rowan eventually learns from Tessa that she knew his father had been declared a deserter, but not the full extent of his earlier conduct. She repeated the heroic story to protect the children and herself from its uncertainty.

Nobody supplies a single comforting verdict. Rowan has to decide how to love someone without keeping a false version of them intact.

**Chapter 6: The friend at the door — Merrow Abbey.** The abbey offers neutral ground for displaced communities and opponents of Veyr. It has already appeared as a place of refuge and correspondence, rather than being introduced solely to be betrayed.

At a council, one rebel leader proposes using the Crown to force the capital to surrender. Rowan, supported by Ysra, rejects the plan. Cael nevertheless sees proof that someone will eventually seize it, and that merely defeating Veyr will not end the struggle for power.

Cael makes contact with Veyr's envoy. He is offered a formal place in the new army and protection for Brackenford's survivors. His deepest motive is not a secret illness or magical compulsion: he wants somebody powerful to guarantee that their loss will never happen again, even at an intolerable cost to strangers.

Entrusted with guarding the Crown during preparations to move it, he asks Orin to accompany him to check its transport seals. He instead brings both to Veyr's waiting agents, using their trust to arrange the handover. When Rowan catches up, Cael closes the abbey's ward gate between them and openly chooses Veyr. A scene makes the betrayal explicit; it is not disguised as an unwinnable battle in which player effort secretly cannot matter.

Royal forces occupy the abbey. Rowan has a chance to chase Cael but instead stays to lead the refugees out—the decision he failed to make during the prison escape. The party loses its captured advantage, its scholar and its safe meeting place. Cael knows that Orin faces coercion and imprisonment. His regret does not erase his responsibility.

### Act III — The people who remain

**Chapter 7: No one's banner — return and open roads.** The survivors regroup. Rowan visits what remains of Brackenford with Tessa. Mira's unfinished classroom cannot simply be restored into the life she would have had. A later community project may honour it, but grief does not become a construction reward.

The party has two military problems: get people into the capital and obtain cooperation from soldiers who can reach the regimental standards. Two regional routes can now be completed in either order:

- **The Glass Coast:** Nessa negotiates with independent captains. They want a credible evacuation plan, not a demand to sacrifice their communities for a glorious assault. A coastal expedition recovers a missing pilot and establishes a usable approach through the reefs. Nessa becomes an organiser by putting responsibilities on paper and keeping them.
- **The upland garrisons:** Bram is ordered to prepare his regiment for the coronation's final binding. He refuses and helps the party evacuate soldiers who want to leave. His choice happens before an offer of party membership. He joins after surrendering command and accepting that some survivors will never welcome him.

Neither route requires a fishing level, a crafted ship or a trade commission. Exploration supplies routes, relationships and knowledge. Optional caves, fishing grounds, hunting contracts and local stories sit alongside these main objectives.

**Chapter 8: The price of certainty — the capital's outer wards.** Veyr is consolidating his coronation. The final muster cannot happen until his commanders and standards arrive, providing an intelligible preparation window. The player triggers the assault when ready; the story does not announce that it starts in an hour and then permit weeks of errands.

The party infiltrates the capital to rescue Orin and prepare the release operation. Cael, now serving Veyr, is confronted with plans to extend binding to civilian service. He realises the system is following the logic he accepted, rather than departing from an otherwise innocent promise.

He helps Orin reach the party during their rescue. Their plan already provides another way in; Cael's action matters without making his betrayal a necessary secret route to victory. He admits that he chose a guarantee over his friends and then called that choice duty.

Rowan can express anger, limited willingness to hear him, or hope for eventual reconciliation. None automatically restores party membership, public trust or absolution. Cael offers testimony and helps protect an evacuation route. He does not purchase forgiveness through a convenient heroic death.

Orin brings the exact disposition of the standards, obtained during his captivity. The release rite itself was already learned at Harrowfield. This is operational information, not a new magic solution invented in the last chapter.

**Chapter 9: The first crown, the last command — the coronation citadel.** Veyr's muster gathers the bound regiments and their standards in one fortified place. The ceremony that would consolidate his power also makes a coordinated release possible.

The allied fleet and defecting soldiers hold routes and evacuate civilians. The playable party enters the citadel through the approach established earlier. Earlier companions and regional decisions have practical payoffs without requiring an enormous on-screen battle.

The final encounter has three dramatic phases:

1. **Reach the bearer.** The party confronts Veyr while the standards sustain his protection. Protecting the people helping with the release matters alongside damage. Previous fights have already taught protection, interruption and handling a marked opponent.
2. **Break the commands.** Veyr draws the bound strength into an armoured sovereign form. The party opens opportunities to release one standard at a time. Each released regiment stops contributing to his power. This is Veyr using the Crown, not another villain arriving to replace him.
3. **The man underneath.** With the bindings severed, Veyr can no longer claim to embody the whole army. He refuses surrender and fights with his own established battle arts. The party defeats him; he dies in that confrontation. The unbound Crown is then destroyed safely.

Rowan's final decision is to finish the release before pursuing the exposed Veyr, trusting his companions to keep them alive. It is the reversal of his mistake at the prison. Victory comes from what the party has learned and become, not an unexplained surge of protagonist power.

**Chapter 10: The road after — a playable epilogue.** The armies do not become friends simply because their controller is dead. Civilian and regional representatives begin a difficult settlement. The ending establishes a viable direction and local consequences without pretending every political problem has vanished.

Ysra helps patients recover. Nessa restores an independent route and chooses who she now calls part of her circle. Orin publishes the records under his own name, including his failures. Bram testifies about his service and accepts losing his rank. Cael faces an inquiry and the people harmed by his choice; his relationship with Rowan remains changed even if there is a possibility of repair.

Rowan can finally speak about Mira as a person rather than only the reason he is fighting. He starts travelling by choice instead of as an escape or a pursuit. The concluding journey returns to the river crossing, where life continues with a visible absence.

The main conflict is complete. An epilogue state permits remaining trade projects, optional hunts and local stories, with affected dialogue updated. It should not require pretending that Veyr is still awaiting the final battle.

## 7. Backstory as discoveries, not a single exposition scene

| What the player initially believes | Early evidence | Later discovery | How it changes a decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| A rogue officer caused Brackenford. | An official seizure order; the magistrate refuses to investigate. | Veyr authorised the clearance and suppresses its consequences. | The objective grows from prosecution to stopping the system protecting him. |
| Rowan's father was an uncomplicated hero. | A monument's account does not match a survivor's memory. | He obeyed harmful orders before resisting and dying for that resistance. | Rowan stops treating uniforms and reputations as sufficient moral answers. |
| Ysra was merely passing through. | Mira knows her; refugees recognise her. | They had an existing network for sheltering patients and witnesses. | Rowan learns that Mira made consequential choices he never noticed. |
| Cael is simply the steadier friend. | He admires decisive command, challenges Rowan's recklessness and listens to the envoy. | He believes coercive peace is preferable to the risk of free conflict. | The betrayal becomes the result of a developing conviction, not a random twist. |
| Destroying the artefact ends the danger. | Ysra describes patients harmed by damaged test equipment; recovered records document the danger of breaking the Crown. | An older release procedure works, but scaling it requires the standards. | The party must pursue a harder victory that protects those caught in the system. |
| Every enemy soldier chose the same thing. | Some follow orders willingly; others visibly struggle under compulsion. | Bram has experienced both and is responsible for distinguishing them. | The party learns to oppose an army without flattening everyone inside it into one category. |

Characters share material facts when they learn them. Suspense should come from incomplete evidence, disputed interpretation and difficult choices—not from a companion withholding an obvious explanation for several chapters without a reason.

## 8. Optional trades: a substantial second progression path

The main campaign supplies the experience, equipment, money and learned abilities needed to finish it. Trades reward curiosity and investment through additional options, powerful specialised items, collections, access to optional content and visible achievement.

They can have their own stories. An ambitious apprentice, a feud between fishing families, a ruined forge and an eccentric collector can matter without turning the central conflict into a resource-gathering plot.

### Example progression

| Trade | Early satisfaction | Developed play | Mastery reward and optional adventure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mining** | Read seams and choose how to extract ore without spoiling a fragile find. Sell it or save it. | New tools and survey knowledge open deposits and abandoned workings. | Find an unusual alloy source in an optional crystal cavern; a survey journal and distinctive pick record mastery. |
| **Smithing** | Make a useful modification and understand its effect before committing materials. | Choose properties, refurbish discoveries and complete named commissions. | Restore a fallen master's forge and create a signature weapon with a distinctive tactical effect and appearance. |
| **Fishing** | Learn habitats, choose tackle and complete a small local collection. | Catch behaviour and equipment create new decisions; catches can be sold, cooked or displayed. | Follow a fisher's accounts to a hidden lake, earn unusual tackle and complete a legendary-catch challenge. |
| **Woodcutting and carpentry** | Choose useful timber and make a small item with a visible result. | Recognise species, plan cuts and build furnishings or specialised equipment parts. | Restore an optional lodge or craft an exceptional bow component; unlock a woodland side story. |
| **Foraging and alchemy** | Identify ingredients and make dependable recovery supplies. | Discover interactions, improve yield and make preparation choices for particular enemies. | Complete a botanist's expedition and create rare tactical mixtures; gain access to an optional garden collection. |

The mastery quests above are candidate content, not commitments to build every trade immediately. Early activities should remain enjoyable before their reward trees grow.

### Rewards need to be worth having

Some crafted items may be among the strongest choices for particular builds. Optional does not mean cosmetically irrelevant. Balance the main campaign around accessible equipment and use harder optional encounters to give exceptional items somewhere to shine.

For example, a master-forged sword might convert a successful counter into an ally's opportunity; a specially constructed bow could reward repeated attacks on a marked weak point. These change play. A collection cabinet, named tool, renovated workshop or NPC recognising the player's workmanship also makes progress visible outside the inventory.

Trade quests can unlock rare vendors, small locations, recipes, challenge encounters and personal stories. They never contain the only release rite, the only way into the capital, the only explanation for Cael's choice or the requirement for saving bound soldiers.

### Avoid turning achievement into chores

- Use observation and decisions in unfamiliar situations; allow reliable batch work for mastered routine tasks.
- Separate story time from real-world timers. The player should not have to return at a particular hour or wait five minutes for ordinary ore.
- Offer forgiving controls and a turn-based alternative to reflex-dependent activities.
- Let materials come from more than one route where practical: gathering, treasure, shops or commissions. Do not force fishing to progress smithing.
- Make known material uses and selling consequences visible. Protect unique quest items from accidental sale.
- Keep trade expertise on the player/profile rather than an ally who can leave during the story.
- Clearly pause free exploration during a short emergency, then restore it. Do not punish the player for believing the game when it offers downtime.

## 9. Exploration and progression before each dramatic test

| Story moment | Player preparation required | Exploration's purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Brackenford's escape | Navigation, interacting with people, basic cooperation, understandable checkpoint recovery. | Establish a place and relationships that the player will later remember. |
| Island-prison breakout | Healing, guarding, inspecting a route and reading an objective. | Discover a path and meet prisoners whose existence changes the goal. |
| Rookspine and taking the Crown | Party swapping, enemy status information and interrupting a charged action. | Expose the difference between official rooms, hidden experiments and prisoners' accounts. |
| Harrowfield's revelations | A journal that distinguishes evidence from assumptions; time to process companion reactions. | Read a ruined place through both physical details and surviving records. |
| Cael's betrayal | A developed relationship with him and earlier value conflicts. | The abbey must already feel like somewhere the party could safely return. |
| Recruiting support | Familiarity with each ally's priorities and an opportunity to choose regional order. | Show a broader world with its own concerns, not merely a chain of villain facilities. |
| Final release operation | Every required battle interaction rehearsed in smaller fights; sufficient main-path equipment. | Earlier routes, rescued people and learned information become useful. |
| Epilogue | A clear victory and accurate state changes for completed chapters. | Let the player revisit what their actions changed and finish optional interests. |

The betrayal must not confiscate expensive player investment. When Cael leaves, unique gear returns to shared inventory and shared progression keeps the remaining party viable. Bram has his own identity; he is not a statistical punishment or a simple replacement skin.

## 10. Continuity checks and risks to resolve in the next draft

**Why attack this town?** Ysra's established refuge network leads there; a military investigation tracks her. Rowan is caught in other people's choices, not selected by a prophecy.

**Why do the companions stay?** Their initial needs lead into a shared threat. Kellan is rescued early; he is not repeatedly kidnapped to keep Nessa participating. Later loyalty develops through shared responsibility.

**Why does Veyr not instantly bind everybody?** Marks require preparation, commands require the Crown and standards, and political/military control is incomplete. His muster is the culmination of a plan, not a power he inexplicably neglected to use earlier.

**Why do the heroes not just smash the Crown?** They learn the fatal consequence before they have a safe opportunity to do so, verify an alternative, and pursue that alternative. The final release is the payoff to their investigation.

**Why does Cael betray people he loves?** He chooses a promised system over their judgement. The argument with Rowan is genuine, but a legitimate alternative exists; betrayal remains his choice. No late possession twist absolves him.

**Why does the finale need this party?** Ysra understands the patients and release procedure; Orin understands the standards and records; Nessa makes the approach and evacuation possible; Bram obtains soldiers' cooperation; Rowan has learned to coordinate and trust them. None requires secret royal ancestry.

**Is the beginning too familiar?** A destroyed home is a familiar premise. The draft must earn it through specific playable relationships, and carry Mira's personhood beyond the attack. If the opening feels like a generic revenge tutorial in a scene draft, it needs revision rather than additional tragedy.

**Does a complicated artefact overwhelm the human story?** The five rules must become understandable through a few scenes and encounters. If they need repeated lectures, simplify the magic rather than add more exceptions.

**Is Cael's shift believable?** The scene draft must show warmth, ideological movement, a chance to choose differently and a concrete betrayal. Foreshadowing should be recognisable in retrospect without making him obviously treacherous from his first appearance.

**What is still undecided?** Exact chapter length, visual identity, scene dialogue, optional romances, the depth of each trade and final combat numbers. The next writing task is a playable opening outline and key relationship scenes, followed by a full beat sheet. This treatment is a coherent candidate for discussion, not a finished screenplay or a locked production scope.
